# Questions

## English has more derivational than inflectional morphemes.

- ✅ True
- ❌ False

**Explanation**: English has a rich system of derivational affixes (prefixes and suffixes) that create new lexemes, while inflectional morphemes are limited in number (e.g., plural, past tense, genitive, comparative, superlative).


## Derivational morphemes produce word-forms of a single lexeme.

- ❌ True
- ✅ False

**Explanation**: This is incorrect. Derivational morphemes create new lexemes (e.g., *sing* → *singer* creates a new lexeme). It is inflectional morphemes that produce different word-forms of the same lexeme (e.g., *singer* → *singers*).


## Which of the following best describes the relationship between *singers* and *singer*?

- ❌ They are different lexemes
- ✅ *singers* is a word-form of the lexeme *singer*
- ❌ *singer* is a word-form of the lexeme *singers*
- ❌ They are unrelated words

**Explanation**: *Singers* is an inflected form (plural) of the lexeme *singer*. The lexeme *singer* has word-forms like *singer* (singular) and *singers* (plural).


## English has no inflectional prefixes.

- ✅ True
- ❌ False

**Explanation**: All inflectional morphemes in English are suffixes (e.g., plural {-s}, past tense {-ed}, genitive {'s}, comparative {-er}, superlative {-est}). There are no inflectional prefixes in English.


## The plural morpheme in *boxes* (/ˈbɒksɪz/) is an example of:

- ✅ A phonologically conditioned allomorph
- ❌ A lexically conditioned allomorph
- ❌ A grammatically conditioned allomorph
- ❌ An irregular allomorph

**Explanation**: The allomorph /ɪz/ appears after sibilant sounds (/s/, /z/, /ʃ/, /ʒ/, /tʃ/, /dʒ/) due to phonological rules. This is phonologically conditioned allomorphy.


## The majority of productive English affixes are non-Germanic.

- ✅ True
- ❌ False

**Explanation**: Many productive English affixes come from Latin, Greek, or French origins (e.g., {re-}, {pre-}, {tion}, {ity}), reflecting the historical influence of these languages on English vocabulary.


## All derivational affixes of English change word-class.

- ❌ True
- ✅ False

**Explanation**: While many derivational affixes change word-class (e.g., *-er*: V>N, *-ly*: Adj>Adv), some do not (e.g., *un-*: Adj>Adj in *happy* > *unhappy*, *-hood*: N>N in *child* > *childhood*).


## In the word *removals*, what is the root?

- ❌ *removal*
- ❌ *move*
- ❌ *removals*
- ✅ *remove*

**Explanation**: The root is the minimal lexical unit that cannot be morphologically analysed further. In *removals*, removing all affixes ({-s}, {-al}) leaves *remove* as the root. *remove* was borrowed as one unit from French.


## The plural morpheme in *two sheep* is an example of:

- ✅ A zero morpheme
- ❌ An irregular allomorph
- ❌ A portmanteau morpheme
- ❌ A phonologically conditioned allomorph

**Explanation**: In *two sheep*, the plural is expressed by a zero morpheme (no phonetic substance). The word form is identical to the singular.
